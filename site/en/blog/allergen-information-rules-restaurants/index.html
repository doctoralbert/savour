Allergen information in restaurants: what the rules ask in the UK and EU

Every guest has the right to know which of the 14 allergens are in their dish, even when nothing is packaged. How you tell them is partly up to you. These are the rules, and this is how to keep them right per recipe.

SAVOUR

23 November 2026 · by Jop Schreurs · 5 min read

In short

  • For food that is not prepacked you still have to name the 14 allergens: in restaurants, catering, bakeries and canteens.
  • In the UK you may give it verbally, with a clearly visible written notice telling guests to ask.
  • The information is only as good as your recipes, including base recipes such as stocks, doughs and sauces.

The 14 allergens are the same in the UK and the EU

Both the UK and the EU work with the same 14 allergens. In the EU they come from the food information regulation (Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011), which every member state applies with its own national rules on how the information is given. The UK kept the same list after leaving the EU. The rule applies not only to packaged products but to everything you sell or serve unpackaged: the dish in your restaurant, the sandwich over the counter, the meal in the canteen.

The 14 allergens

These are the 14 allergens, with examples of where they are easily missed in the kitchen.

Always check your supplier’s label: the same product can differ by brand.

The rules in the UK

The Food Standards Agency (FSA) guidance for England, Wales and Northern Ireland says you must supply allergen information for every item that contains any of the 14 allergens, including meals in restaurants and takeaway food.

You may give it in writing, for example on the menu, a chalkboard or in an information pack. You may also give it verbally, as long as a written notice in a clearly visible place tells customers how to get the information. The FSA adds that written information supported by a conversation works best for guests. At a buffet, the information is needed per dish. For delivery and takeaway ordered at a distance, guests need it before they buy and again when the food arrives.

Natasha’s Law: food packed on your own premises

Food you pack on the same premises where you sell it, before the customer orders, is called prepacked for direct sale (PPDS): the sandwich you make in the morning and put in the fridge, for example. Since October 2021 this food needs a full label with the name and the full ingredients list, with the 14 allergens emphasised. This is known as Natasha’s Law. Food you pack only after the customer orders falls under the normal rules for non-prepacked food.

In the EU

In the EU every country decides how the information may be given. Some allow verbal information with a written record and a notice, others ask for written information. Working in an EU country? Check the rules of that country: our Dutch, German, Spanish and French articles each explain the local rules.

Your recipes are the foundation

An allergen list is only as good as the recipe behind it. Three things often go wrong:

Base recipes. The celery is not in the dish, but in the stock you use in it. Look only at the dish and you miss it.

Substitutions. The supplier delivers a different brand of mustard or stock cube, with something else in it. The recipe stays the same, the allergens do not.

An updated recipe. The chef adds pesto, but the allergen list at the till stays the same. Update the list as soon as the recipe changes.

One place for recipe and allergens

Keep the allergens with the recipe itself, not in a separate spreadsheet. Then one changes when the other does.

What you want to be able to show

An inspector wants to see how you know what you tell your guests. Make sure you can show per dish which allergens it contains and what that is based on: your recipes and your suppliers’ labels or specifications. And make sure every member of staff knows where that information is, even on a busy night.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers to what chefs ask most.

Do I have to put allergens on the menu?

Not in the UK. Verbal information is allowed, as long as a clearly visible written notice tells guests to ask. Many kitchens still put them in writing, because the FSA says that works best.

Does this apply to catering and canteens too?

Yes. It applies to everyone who supplies food that is not prepacked to consumers, including caterers, canteens and bakeries.

What about “may contain”?

The 14 allergens are about what you use as an ingredient. Precautionary statements about cross-contact are a separate topic; your local authority can tell you what applies to your business.

Is this legal advice?

No. This article explains the rules as the authorities describe them. If you are unsure about your situation, ask your local authority or the FSA.
